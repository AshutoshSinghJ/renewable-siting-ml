Which renewable fits here?

10,724 European solar and wind farm sites. Can geography alone tell them apart?

Results

Works where wind farms are much windier than solar farms

The Random Forest was tested on Spain and Portugal, on each European region in turn, and on the USA, China and India, every time on data it never saw.

One test region is not enough

Why the British Isles fail

Solar farms in the UK and Ireland stand at a median wind speed of 7.7 m/s, windier than the typical wind farm elsewhere in Europe (7.4 m/s). The model learned “windy means wind farm” in absolute terms, so it calls almost every British solar farm a wind farm.

Other continents: trained on Europe, tested unchanged

The USA works because, as in southern Europe, its wind farms stand in far windier places than its solar farms (8.1 against 5.7 m/s). China scores 84%, but 81% of its sites are wind farms, so always guessing Wind is nearly as good, and none of its 40 Hybrid cells are found. India fails because its wind farms sit at only 6.4 m/s, below what counts as windy in Europe; but India is far sunnier than anything in training, so the model answers “I don't know” for 92% of Indian cells.

Is it overfitting?

Not badly. A model that memorises its data scores near 100% on it; this one scores 81%, and 73% on unseen blocks. Iberia scores higher because it is an easy region: even a one-line wind rule gets 88% there.

Iberia: what the model predicted

Solar and Wind are about 92% right. Hybrid fails: 18 of 25 are called Wind.

Saying “I don't know”

Abstaining on low confidence works. The “unfamiliar place” rule is too cautious: those cells were mostly right.

What drives the predictions

Mean SHAP value per feature: how far each one moves the predicted probabilities. Wind speed dominates, sunshine comes second.

The question

Given a place where a renewable farm exists, can a model tell from geography alone whether it is solar, wind or hybrid, even in a region it has never seen, and say “I don't know” when unsure?

It answers “which type fits here if something is built?”, never “should anything be built here?”.

How it works

  1. 01 · LABELSReal farms

    24,078 utility-scale farms from OpenStreetMap (Dunnett et al. 2020), grouped into 5 km cells: Solar, Wind or Hybrid.

  2. 02 · FEATURESFive layers

    Sunshine, wind speed at 100 m, elevation, slope and distance to the grid, sampled at each farm.

  3. 03 · MODELRandom Forest

    Compared with a majority guess, a wind-speed rule and logistic regression.

  4. 04 · TESTSUnseen regions

    Scored on Spain and Portugal once, then on each European region in turn, then on the USA, China and India.

Validation vs test

Validation

Spatial cross-validation inside the training region: 5 folds of whole 100 km blocks. Used only to choose model settings.

Test

Spain and Portugal, scored once at the end, never used for tuning.

Extra

Each of 8 regions held out in turn with the same settings, then the Europe-trained model applied unchanged to the USA, China and India.

Using the model on a new location

ASSUMPTION

If existing farms reflect well-informed siting decisions, the model recommends which type (Solar, Wind or Hybrid) fits a location if it is developed, and says “I don't know” when it is out of its depth.

Why it is reasonable

Developers study wind, sun, terrain and grid access before building, so real farms carry expert judgement. Sachit et al. (2022) uses the same idea.

Why it is only partly true

Land prices, subsidies and permits matter too: UK solar farms stand in windy places. Accuracy ranges from 35% (British Isles) to 92% (Italy).

How to use it

Read it as “which type fits if something is built”, never “build here”: the model never saw unsuitable land. Respect “I don't know”.

Limitations

  • Absolute wind speed does not transfer where wind farms are not much windier than solar farms: the UK (35%) and India (53%).
  • Few Hybrid examples (333), most of them in Germany.
  • OpenStreetMap coverage is uneven, and the data is a 2020 snapshot.
  • Type, not suitability. The model never says where to build.

Next steps

  • Use relative wind speed (compared with the surrounding region) so the model transfers to windy countries.
  • Add Hybrid examples from other continents.
  • Calibrate the unfamiliar-location threshold.
  • Cross-check labels with Global Energy Monitor.
